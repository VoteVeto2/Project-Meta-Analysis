More Agents, Better Results? A Meta-Analysis of Multi-Agent vs Single-Agent LLM Systems

Xinyu Yang (r1020926)
KU Leuven, Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences
Meta-Analysis (G0B75a), Academic Year 2025–2026
22 May 2026 — Revision 5 (final, post v4 audit)

Abstract

This exploratory convenience-sample meta-analysis synthesizes 41 within-study comparisons (2023–2026) of multi-agent LLM orchestration versus single-agent prompting on benchmark accuracy. Under a REML random-effects model with full Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment, the pooled log-OR was 0.45, 95% CI [0.23, 0.67], p < .001 (OR = 1.57; approximate risk difference ≈ 3–5 percentage points). However, this estimate is fragile. Heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 96.1%, Q(40) = 1016.6), the 95% prediction interval crossed zero ([−0.74, 1.64]), Egger's test detected significant funnel asymmetry (p = .024), and the overall Cohen's h was trivial (h = 0.08). Under compute parity (k = 9), the effect attenuated to log-OR = 0.31 (h ≈ 0.05, negligible). No meta-regression moderator reached significance (QM(8) = 5.34, p = .72; R² = 0%). Subgroup analyses suggest the effect may concentrate in code-generation tasks and hierarchical architectures, but between-group tests (task: Qbetween(5) = 197.56, p < .001; architecture: Qbetween(4) = 32.75, p < .001) should be interpreted cautiously given uncontrolled confounds. With 61% of studies rated high risk of bias, only 10 of 41 rows independently verified (76% unverified), and evidence of publication bias, these findings are provisional and hypothesis-generating. Multi-agent orchestration probably helps for decomposable tasks under generous compute budgets, but the evidence base is too contaminated to support strong claims.

1. Introduction

The multi-agent paradigm for large language models rests on a compelling intuition: just as human teams outperform individuals on complex tasks, multiple LLM instances collaborating through structured protocols should exceed what a single instance achieves alone. Early work by Du et al. (2023) demonstrated that multiagent debate improved factuality and reasoning in ChatGPT, while Liang et al. (2024) showed that multi-agent discussion encouraged divergent thinking. Frameworks such as AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023), MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2023), and ChatDev (Qian et al., 2024) now orchestrate dozens of specialized agents in hierarchies, debates, and cooperative workflows.

Yet a skeptical counter-narrative has emerged. Sprague et al. (2024) argue that much of the observed multi-agent advantage dissolves once compute is equalized — multi-agent systems consume more inference tokens, and scaling a single agent's sampling budget may match multi-agent performance. Zhang et al. (2025) directly show that single-agent baselines with equivalent token budgets frequently match or outperform multi-agent debate. As frontier models improve, the coordination overhead may outweigh diminishing marginal gains.

No meta-analysis has yet synthesized these conflicting findings. Individual studies vary in architecture (debate vs. hierarchy vs. cooperation), task domain (code vs. medical vs. math), backbone model, and whether compute parity is enforced. This heterogeneity makes narrative reviews unreliable. We address four research questions:

RQ1 (Primary). Do multi-agent LLM systems outperform single-agent baselines on benchmark accuracy?
RQ2 (Compute parity). Under matched compute budgets, does the multi-agent advantage survive?
RQ3 (Frontier paradox). As baseline single-agent accuracy increases, does the orchestration gain shrink?
RQ4 (Moderators). Which task and architecture properties predict effect direction?

2. Methods

2.1 Search strategy and limitations

We deployed six parallel search agents covering distinct topic clusters: multi-agent debate, cooperative/hierarchical architectures, SWE and code generation, medical/scientific reasoning, critical and null-result literature, and frontier-model comparisons. Each agent queried arXiv, ACL Anthology, NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR proceedings, and Google Scholar, targeting papers from 2023–2026. This yielded 153 records. Search strings are documented in Appendix C.

Important caveat: This is a convenience sample, not a registered systematic review. No protocol was pre-registered. The search strategy, while broad, cannot guarantee exhaustive coverage. The six-agent approach introduces selection biases that differ from but are not necessarily better than manual systematic searching. Results should be treated as hypothesis-generating.

2.2 Screening and inclusion (PICOS)

Population: Empirical 2023–2026 LLM studies. Intervention: Any multi-agent orchestration (debate, cooperation, hierarchy, role-play, verifier-critic, mixture-of-agents, planner-executor). Comparator: Single-agent baseline using the same backbone model. Outcome: Accuracy or success-rate proportion on a named benchmark. Study design: Within-study head-to-head comparison.

Exclusions: classical multi-agent reinforcement learning, position papers without empirical results, studies lacking a single-agent comparator, and simulation-only evaluations. After deduplication (26 removed) and PICOS screening (21 excluded), 106 records remained. Of these, 41 reported extractable accuracy data for both multi-agent and single-agent conditions. The 65 excluded records are catalogued in Appendix D. Screening was performed by the author with AI assistance; no formal inter-rater reliability was computed, which is a limitation.

2.3 Effect size

We computed log odds ratios (log-OR) as the primary effect size. A 0.5 continuity correction was applied conditionally, only to studies with zero cells (Sweeting et al., 2004); none were present. For each study, we extracted n-items (total benchmark questions), n-correct under multi-agent, and n-correct under single-agent conditions. Where n-items could not be extracted directly, it was estimated from reported percentages and total benchmark sizes. Of the 41 studies, 18 carry a broad n-items-estimated flag (any form of derivation); a stricter classification by source quality identifies 13 with imputed or estimated denominators (k = 28 exact). The sensitivity analysis in Section 3.9 uses the stricter definition. Cohen's h was computed as a secondary effect size to provide absolute-scale context (see Appendix E).

Risk-difference approximation. To aid practical interpretation, we report approximate risk differences (RD = pMA − pSA) alongside log-OR. The pooled RD across all 41 studies is approximately 3–5 percentage points, meaning multi-agent systems solve roughly 3–5 additional items per 100 on average. Under compute parity, this shrinks to approximately 2 percentage points.

Metric heterogeneity. The 41 comparisons include accuracy, pass@1, win rate, executability scores, and resolve rates. We treated all as binomial proportions, but acknowledge this conflation is a simplification. Win-rate benchmarks (e.g., AlpacaEval) operate under different assumptions than item-level accuracy; results should be interpreted with this caveat.

2.4 Statistical model

The primary analysis used a restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) random-effects model (Viechtbauer, 2005) with a modified Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment (Hartung & Knapp, 2001; IntHout et al., 2014). The HKSJ adjustment computes the scale factor s²HK = Σwi(yi − μ)²/(k−1) and applies max(1, s²HK) as a variance inflation floor, following the conservative recommendation that HKSJ should never narrow CIs relative to the naïve inverse-variance estimate (Röver et al., 2015). CIs use t(k−1) degrees of freedom. Prediction intervals use t(k−2) following IntHout et al. (2016). DerSimonian-Laird estimation served as a sensitivity check. Cochran's Q is computed using fixed-effect weights and the fixed-effect pooled mean, yielding a single Q value identical across variance estimators (Borenstein et al., 2009, Ch. 16).

Subgroup analyses used separate random-effects models per level with Qbetween computed via the partition-of-Q approach (Qbetween = Qtotal − ΣQwithin; Borenstein et al., 2009, Ch. 19). Meta-regression was mixed-effects with conditional REML τ² estimation and Knapp-Hartung t-distribution inference, reporting the omnibus QM test (Viechtbauer, 2010). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses re-estimate τ² per iteration.

Dependency. Multiple effect sizes sometimes derive from the same paper, benchmark family, or model family. We did not implement robust variance estimation (Hedges et al., 2010; Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022) or multilevel meta-analysis (Van den Noortgate et al., 2013) in this version. The non-aggregated sensitivity (k = 36) partially addresses but does not solve this limitation.

2.5 Risk of bias

We adapted the ROB 2.0 framework (Sterne et al., 2019) with domains for selection, performance/compute parity, reporting, and attrition. Of 41 studies, 25 (61%) were rated high risk of bias, predominantly due to uncontrolled compute: multi-agent systems typically consumed more inference tokens without any single-agent scaling comparison. Of the 41 extracted rows, only 10 are independently verified (4 verified, 6 verified-corrected); 2 are unverifiable (source data could not be located); 29 remain unaudited. Among the 12 rows that were audited, 6 required correction (50% error rate). Because 31 of 41 rows (76%) lack independent verification, the pooled estimate is substantially exposed to extraction error.

2.6 Publication bias

Funnel plot inspection, Egger's regression test (Egger et al., 1997), Begg's rank correlation (Begg & Mazumdar, 1994), Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill (Duval & Tweedie, 2000), and p-curve analysis (Simonsohn et al., 2014) were conducted with k = 41.

3.2 Descriptive characteristics

The 41 included comparisons span 2023–2026. The dominant architecture is debate (k = 20, 49%), followed by cooperation (k = 9), hierarchy (k = 6), verifier-critic (k = 2), and role-play (k = 2), with one mixture-of-agents and one planner-executor study. Task categories include code generation (k = 11), "other" mixed benchmarks (k = 10), medical reasoning (k = 7), math (k = 6), evaluation tasks (k = 3), general knowledge (k = 3), and factuality (k = 1). Backbone models span GPT-4 variants, Claude, Gemini, Qwen, and open-source models. Of the 41 studies, 18 have estimated (rather than directly extracted) n-items values. Only 9 studies enforced compute parity between multi-agent and single-agent conditions.

The REML model yields a pooled log-OR of 0.45 (95% CI [0.23, 0.67], p < .001), corresponding to OR = 1.57. On an absolute scale, however, the overall Cohen's h is 0.08 — well below the 0.20 threshold for a "small" effect (Cohen, 1988). The approximate risk difference of 3–5 percentage points means that multi-agent systems solve roughly 3–5 additional items per 100 benchmark questions. Whether this justifies the added complexity and compute cost is a system-design question, not a statistical one.

Critical caveat: multiple threats undermine this estimate. The pooled effect is positive but fragile: (1) The prediction interval [−0.74, 1.64] crosses zero, so a new study could plausibly find single-agent superiority. (2) Egger's test is significant (p = .024), indicating funnel asymmetry and possible publication bias or small-study effects. (3) Cohen's h = 0.08 is trivial on the absolute scale. (4) 61% of studies are high-RoB and 76% of rows lack independent verification. (5) I² = 96.1% means the "average effect" is nearly meaningless as a point prediction. The honest interpretation is that this is a positive but provisional signal, not a robust finding.

3.4 Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity is extreme: I² = 96.1%, Q(40) = 1016.6 (p < .001), τ² = 0.34, τ = 0.58. The between-study standard deviation (τ = 0.58) exceeds the pooled estimate itself (0.45), confirming that the distribution of true effects is wide. The pooled mean should not be used as a point prediction for any specific setting.

Publication bias is now a concern. Egger's regression test, computed against the intercept, is significant (intercept = 2.53, t(39) = 2.34, p = .024). This indicates funnel asymmetry consistent with small-study effects — smaller studies in the sample tend to show larger positive effects. This pattern is characteristic of publication bias (small positive-result studies are published while small null-result studies are not) or genuine heterogeneity correlated with study size. The Begg test and trim-and-fill are non-significant, but these methods have lower sensitivity than Egger's test with continuous outcomes. The p-curve shows right-skewness (25/28 significant results), but p-curve assumes independent, homogeneous hypothesis tests — conditions that are only approximately met here. Overall, the positive pooled estimate may be inflated by selective reporting, and this substantially weakens the main finding.

The most consequential sensitivity is compute parity (k = 9): restricting to studies that equalized inference tokens reduces the pooled log-OR from 0.45 to 0.31 (a ~31% attenuation). The CI lower bound barely escapes zero (unrounded: [0.006, 0.607]), and the corresponding Cohen's h is approximately 0.05 — negligible by conventional benchmarks. This means multi-agent systems under fair compute comparisons gain roughly 2 additional correct items per 100 benchmark questions, an effect that is statistically borderline and practically trivial.

The verified-only subset (k = 10) is notably lower than the full-sample estimate: log-OR = 0.35 [0.19, 0.51], with sharply reduced heterogeneity (τ² = 0.01 vs. 0.34). The lower point estimate is a warning: the 31 unverified studies may contain extraction errors that inflate the pooled effect. The reduced τ² suggests some apparent heterogeneity in the full sample arises from extraction noise. Removing only the 2 unverifiable studies (S013, S027) barely changes the estimate (k = 39, log-OR = 0.43 [0.20, 0.65]), confirming these two rows are not individually problematic but leaving the broader data-quality concern intact.

The exact n-items subset (k = 28) excludes 13 studies whose denominators were imputed or estimated from benchmark metadata, yielding log-OR = 0.40 [0.13, 0.67] — somewhat attenuated from the full sample. The accuracy-only subset (k = 37) excludes non-accuracy metrics (win rates, resolve rates), yielding 0.46 [0.22, 0.71], similar to the full sample. Leave-one-out analysis shows the pooled estimate ranges from 0.40 (omitting Wu2025, a high-influence outlier) to 0.48; no single study reversal changes the sign of the pooled effect.

4. Discussion

4.1 Principal findings

Multi-agent LLM systems appear to outperform single-agent baselines on average (RQ1: log-OR = 0.45), but this statement requires heavy qualification. The prediction interval [−0.74, 1.64] is arguably the more important result: it crosses zero, meaning multi-agent superiority is not guaranteed for any new setting. The Cohen's h of 0.08 provides essential context — on the absolute scale, the "advantage" amounts to roughly 3–5 additional correct answers per 100 benchmark items. For many applications, this may not justify the added infrastructure complexity and inference cost.

The Egger test (p = .024) indicates that this estimate is likely inflated by small-study effects or publication bias. Small studies showing positive results for multi-agent systems are more likely to appear in our sample than small studies showing null results. This asymmetry is a standard threat in meta-analysis and, combined with the other weaknesses, means the true average effect may be smaller than 0.45 — possibly substantially so.

4.2 When might multi-agent work?

The subgroup analyses suggest a pattern, though not a causal conclusion. Code generation (log-OR = 0.83) may benefit because software engineering is inherently modular: generating code, writing tests, reviewing, and debugging map onto distinct agent roles (Hong et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Medical reasoning (log-OR = 0.42) may benefit from adversarial verification, where a second agent challenges the first's diagnosis (Tang et al., 2024). General knowledge shows no benefit (log-OR = 0.02). However, these subgroup differences are confounded with compute allocation, backbone model, and study quality. The significant Qbetween values (task: 197.56; architecture: 32.75) indicate heterogeneity across subgroups but do not establish that task type or architecture type causally moderates the effect.

4.3 The compute confound

Under compute parity, the effect shrinks by ~31% (from 0.45 to 0.31), and the Cohen's h becomes negligible (~0.05). This is the most practically important finding. A multi-agent debate consuming 5x the tokens of a single agent should be compared against a single agent given the same token budget via chain-of-thought, self-consistency, or repeated sampling (Sprague et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). That the multi-agent advantage barely survives after compute equalization, with a CI nearly touching zero ([0.006, 0.61]) and trivial absolute effect size, substantially weakens the practical case for multi-agent orchestration as a general strategy. It may still be justified for specific decomposable tasks where structured inter-agent communication adds genuine informational value beyond what compute scaling alone provides.

4.4 Limitations

Data-quality limitations. First, this is an exploratory convenience-sample meta-analysis, not a registered systematic review. The 65 excluded records without extractable data may not be missing at random (Appendix D). Second, Egger's test is significant (p = .024), indicating publication bias or small-study effects that likely inflate the pooled estimate. Third, 61% of studies are high risk of bias (primarily uncontrolled compute), and only 10 of 41 rows are independently verified (76% unverified), with a 50% correction rate among the 12 audited rows. Fourth, I² = 96.1% is extreme; the pooled mean is nearly meaningless as a point prediction. Fifth, metric heterogeneity (accuracy, pass@1, win rate, resolve rate) means the log-OR model assumes more homogeneity than the underlying data provide.

Analytical limitations. Sixth, dependency among effect sizes is not formally modeled; robust variance estimation (Hedges et al., 2010) or multilevel modeling (Van den Noortgate et al., 2013) would be needed for rigorous inference. Seventh, all subgroup analyses are exploratory and uncorrected for multiplicity. Eighth, no moderator is significant in meta-regression, but the regression is underpowered. Ninth, the actual compute ratios between multi-agent and single-agent conditions are rarely reported, limiting our ability to quantify the confound precisely.

5. Conclusion

This exploratory meta-analysis finds a positive but fragile signal that multi-agent LLM orchestration may outperform single-agent baselines (pooled log-OR = 0.45, 95% CI [0.23, 0.67]; Cohen's h = 0.08; approximate RD = 3–5 percentage points). However, the evidence is substantially undermined by: (1) significant publication bias (Egger p = .024), (2) extreme heterogeneity (I² = 96.1%, PI crossing zero), (3) a trivial absolute effect size, (4) pervasive compute confounding, and (5) weak data provenance (76% unverified, 50% correction rate among audited rows).

The most honest reading is that multi-agent orchestration probably helps for decomposable, verifiable tasks (code generation, medical reasoning) when generous compute budgets are available, but the evidence base is too contaminated by publication bias, compute confounding, and data-quality concerns to make strong claims. Under compute parity, the effect is borderline significant and negligible in absolute terms (h ≈ 0.05). The field urgently needs: (a) compute-controlled comparisons (only 9 of 41 enforced parity), (b) independent extraction audits, and (c) registered meta-analyses with pre-specified moderators. Until then, practitioners should benchmark any multi-agent system against a properly scaled single-agent baseline before assuming orchestration adds value.

Appendix A: Reproducibility

All analyses were conducted using Python 3.12 managed via uv. The full pipeline — from data loading through effect-size computation, pooling, heterogeneity assessment, subgroup analysis, meta-regression, publication bias diagnostics, sensitivity analyses, and figure generation — is executed by a single entry point: 

uv sync && uv run python main.py

Key components:

  • Entry point: main.py runs the full pipeline end-to-end, reading data from data/, executing all analyses via modules in src/, and writing figures and summary outputs to report/assets/.
  • Environment: pyproject.toml + uv.lock for deterministic dependency resolution.
  • Data: Raw search results in data/raw/; extracted and verified effect-size tables in data/extracted/ and data/verified/. The n-items-estimated flag column indicates which studies have estimated rather than directly extracted item counts.
  • Analysis code: Reusable modules in src/ (effect_sizes, pooling, heterogeneity, moderators, publication_bias, sensitivity, plots). All logic is importable.
  • Figures: All figures exported to report/assets/ with stable filenames.
  • Random seeds: Verification sampling used seed 42. All stochastic operations are seeded.
  • Output validation: main.py includes runtime validation checks (k = 41, df = 40, Q > 0, CI/PI bracket the pooled estimate, Egger df = 39). results-summary.json and CSV files in report/assets/ are regenerated by each pipeline run. Table values in this report are sourced from those files.
  • Tests: 18 unit tests in tests/test_stats.py verify DL/REML estimators, HKSJ properties, effect-size computations, Egger's intercept test, and project-specific data constraints. Run via uv run pytest tests/.

Appendix D: Exclusion Table

Of 106 records passing PICOS screening, 65 were excluded because they lacked extractable multi-agent vs. single-agent accuracy counts. The table below categorizes these exclusions. The excluded records may not be missing at random — studies reporting negative or null results may be less likely to provide detailed accuracy tables, introducing potential selection bias. The logical consequence is that the 41 included studies likely overrepresent positive results: if a study found multi-agent systems performed no better (or worse), it was less likely to report the granular accuracy data required for inclusion. Appendix E: Cohen's h as Secondary Effect Size

Cohen's h = 2 arcsin(√pMA) − 2 arcsin(√pSA) provides an absolute-scale effect size that is less sensitive to base-rate inflation than log-OR. In high-accuracy regimes (e.g., >90% accuracy for both conditions), a small proportion difference produces a large log-OR but a small h. Cohen's h benchmarks: 0.20 = small, 0.50 = medium, 0.80 = large (Cohen, 1988).

Table E1. Pooled Cohen's h for key analyses.
AnalysiskCohen's hInterpretation
Full sample410.08Trivial (below "small" threshold)
Code generation110.14Trivial to small
Compute-parity only9≈ 0.05Negligible
Medical reasoning70.09Trivial
General knowledge30.00No effect

Implications. The disconnect between the seemingly encouraging log-OR (0.45, OR = 1.57) and the trivial Cohen's h (0.08) is important. Log-OR is inflated in high-accuracy regimes where both conditions approach ceiling. Many included studies operate in such regimes (e.g., single-agent accuracy >80%), meaning the 57% odds-ratio increase translates to only ~3–5 additional correct items per 100. Under compute parity, this shrinks to ~2 items per 100 (h ≈ 0.05). Practitioners should evaluate multi-agent systems on absolute gains, not odds ratios, when making deployment decisions.

More Agents, Better Results? An Exploratory Convenience-Sample Meta-Analysis of Multi-Agent vs Single-Agent LLM Systems

Xinyu Yang (r1020926)
KU Leuven, Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences
Meta-Analysis (G0B75a), Academic Year 2025–2026
29 May 2026 — Revision 7 (post v6 peer review)

Abstract

This exploratory convenience-sample meta-analysis synthesizes within-study comparisons (2023–2026) of multi-agent LLM orchestration versus single-agent prompting on benchmark accuracy. The primary item-level binomial specification — 37 accuracy-metric studies — gave log-OR = 0.46, 95% CI [0.22, 0.71] (REML with HKSJ); the full convenience sample (k = 41, including four win-/resolve-rate studies) gave log-OR = 0.45 [0.23, 0.67]. On the absolute scale the gain is small and weighting-dependent: an aggregate (precision-weighted) risk difference of ≈3 percentage points (≈8 pp as an unweighted study mean; aggregate Cohen's h = 0.07, unweighted 0.20). The estimate is fragile: heterogeneity is extreme (I² = 96.1%), the prediction interval crosses zero ([−0.74, 1.64]), and Egger's test detects funnel asymmetry (p = .024). Under compute parity (k = 9) the relative effect attenuates (log-OR 0.31) but the absolute gain does not (aggregate RD ≈ 5 pp), because the parity subset omits the large near-null general-knowledge benchmarks. The source-located/verified subset (k = 10) gives a lower log-OR = 0.35 with sharply reduced heterogeneity. Because 76% of rows lack independent verification (50% error rate among audited rows), and most studies did not control compute, these findings are provisional and hypothesis-generating.

1. Introduction

The multi-agent paradigm for large language models rests on a compelling intuition: just as human teams outperform individuals on complex tasks, multiple LLM instances collaborating through structured protocols should exceed what a single instance achieves alone. Early work by Du et al. (2023) demonstrated that multiagent debate improved factuality and reasoning in ChatGPT, while Liang et al. (2024) showed that multi-agent discussion encouraged divergent thinking. Frameworks such as AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023), MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2023), and ChatDev (Qian et al., 2024) now orchestrate dozens of specialized agents in hierarchies, debates, and cooperative workflows.

Yet a skeptical counter-narrative has emerged. Zhang et al. (2025) directly show that multi-agent debate often fails to outperform simple single-agent baselines such as chain-of-thought and self-consistency, even while consuming substantially more inference-time computation. As frontier models improve, the coordination overhead may outweigh diminishing marginal gains.

To our knowledge, no quantitative meta-analysis has yet synthesized these conflicting findings; our non-exhaustive search did not locate a directly comparable study, though multi-agent LLM surveys and benchmarks exist (Guo et al., 2024). Individual studies vary in architecture (debate vs. hierarchy vs. cooperation), task domain (code vs. medical vs. math), backbone model, and whether compute parity is enforced. This heterogeneity makes narrative reviews unreliable. We address four research questions:

RQ1 (Primary). Do multi-agent LLM systems outperform single-agent baselines on benchmark accuracy?
RQ2 (Compute parity). Under matched compute budgets, does the multi-agent advantage survive?
RQ3 (Frontier paradox). As baseline single-agent accuracy increases, does the orchestration gain shrink?
RQ4 (Moderators). Which task and architecture properties predict effect direction?

2. Methods

2.1 Search strategy and limitations

We deployed six parallel search agents covering distinct topic clusters: multi-agent debate, cooperative/hierarchical architectures, SWE and code generation, medical/scientific reasoning, critical and null-result literature, and frontier-model comparisons. Each agent queried arXiv, ACL Anthology, NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR proceedings, and Google Scholar, targeting papers from 2023–2026. This yielded 153 records. Search strings are documented in Appendix C.

Important caveat: This is a convenience sample, not a registered systematic review. No protocol was pre-registered. The search strategy, while broad, cannot guarantee exhaustive coverage. The six-agent approach introduces selection biases that differ from but are not necessarily better than manual systematic searching. Results should be treated as hypothesis-generating.

2.2 Screening and inclusion (PICOS)

Population: Empirical 2023–2026 LLM studies. Intervention: Any multi-agent orchestration (debate, cooperation, hierarchy, role-play, verifier-critic, mixture-of-agents, planner-executor). Comparator: Single-agent baseline using the same backbone model. Outcome: Accuracy or success-rate proportion on a named benchmark. Study design: Within-study head-to-head comparison. Only studies reporting comparable MA and SA outcomes on the same benchmark, using the same or clearly comparable backbone model, were eligible.

Exclusions: classical multi-agent reinforcement learning, position papers without empirical results, studies lacking a single-agent comparator, and simulation-only evaluations. After deduplication (26 removed) and PICOS screening (21 excluded), 106 records remained. Of these, 41 reported extractable accuracy data for both multi-agent and single-agent conditions. The 65 excluded records are catalogued in Appendix D. Screening was performed by the author with AI assistance; no formal inter-rater reliability was computed, which is a limitation.

Two-pass verification. Two distinct processes touched the table, and v7 keeps them separate (they were conflated in v6). (ii) A separate non-blind denominator-normalization pass then replaced the extraction-stage n = 100 placeholder with true benchmark sizes for ~12 further rows; these edits preserve the per-study proportion (and effect-size direction) in all but S002 (flips to a tie) and S023 (proportion not preserved), both flagged for re-extraction. A separate computed axis, n-items-provenance, records this: only 10 rows are paper-audited, 30 are benchmark-imputed (denominator set to the canonical benchmark size), and 1 is percent-estimated. Inter-rater reliability on the 12 blind-audited rows: binary kappa = 0.91; count ICC > 0.95.

Remaining risk. The 29 unaudited rows (71%) may contain similar errors, and 30 of 41 denominators are imputed rather than paper-confirmed. The source-located/verified-only sensitivity analysis (k = 10, Section 3.5) is the most credible anchor for the pooled estimate.

2.4 Effect size

We computed log odds ratios (log-OR) as the primary effect size. Because item-level paired correctness was unavailable, MA and SA proportions were treated as independent binomial counts. Where arms are item-paired (the same benchmark items scored under both conditions), ignoring the positive between-arm correlation overstates each study's within-study variance and is therefore conservative; win-rate and resolve-rate studies are not item-paired. The genuine under-estimation of overall uncertainty arises instead from dependency among effect sizes (multiple comparisons per paper, benchmark, or model family; see §2.5). A 0.5 continuity correction was applied conditionally, only to studies with zero cells (Sweeting et al., 2004); none were present. For each study, we extracted n-items (total benchmark questions), n-correct under multi-agent, and n-correct under single-agent conditions. Where n-items could not be extracted directly, it was set to the canonical benchmark size or estimated from reported percentages. By the computed n-items-provenance axis, only 10 of 41 denominators are paper-audited; 30 are benchmark-imputed and 1 is percent-estimated (the v6 field labelled 28 "exact", but that field conflated paper-confirmed with assumed benchmark sizes). The full extraction table is in Appendix Table A1.

Risk-difference approximation. To aid practical interpretation, we report risk differences (RD = pMA − pSA), computed in-pipeline (src/effect_sizes.pooled_absolute_effect). The RD is weighting-dependent: the aggregate (precision/n-weighted) RD across all 41 studies is ≈3 pp, but the unweighted study mean is ≈8 pp (median 6 pp). Under compute parity the aggregate RD is ≈5 pp — it does not shrink relative to the full sample (§3.4). Log-OR is retained as the formal effect size; the divergence between the relative (log-OR) and absolute (RD) scales across subsets is itself a reason not to over-read any single absolute number.

Metric heterogeneity and the primary specification. The 41 comparisons comprise item-level accuracy (37), one win rate, and three resolve rates. AlpacaEval 2.0 (S040) is a length-controlled pairwise win rate against a GPT-4 reference judged by an LLM annotator — not a per-item binomial accuracy — and the SWE-bench studies (S009, S010, S025) report issue-resolution rates. Treating these four as binomial proportions is a simplification, so the primary specification is the 37 item-level-accuracy studies (k = 37, log-OR = 0.46 [0.22, 0.71]); the full k = 41 is reported as a robustness inclusion. The two are numerically indistinguishable.

2.5 Statistical model

The primary analysis used a restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) random-effects model (Viechtbauer, 2005) with a modified Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment (Hartung & Knapp, 2001; IntHout et al., 2014). The HKSJ adjustment computes the scale factor s²HK = Σwi(yi − μ)²/(k−1) and applies max(1, s²HK) as a variance inflation floor, following the conservative recommendation that HKSJ should never narrow CIs relative to the naïve inverse-variance estimate (Röver et al., 2015). CIs use t(k−1) degrees of freedom. Prediction intervals use t(k−2) following IntHout et al. (2016). DerSimonian-Laird estimation served as a sensitivity check. Cochran's Q is computed using fixed-effect weights and the fixed-effect pooled mean, yielding a single Q value identical across variance estimators (Borenstein et al., 2009, Ch. 16).

Subgroup analyses used separate random-effects models per level with Qbetween computed via the partition-of-Q approach (Qbetween = Qtotal − ΣQwithin; Borenstein et al., 2009, Ch. 19). Singleton categories (factuality k = 1 for task; moa and planner-executor k = 1 each for architecture) were merged into the nearest "other" category so that all subgroup tables sum to k = 41. Meta-regression was mixed-effects with conditional REML τ² estimation and Knapp-Hartung t-distribution inference (Viechtbauer, 2010). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses re-estimate τ² per iteration.

Dependency. Multiple effect sizes sometimes derive from the same paper, benchmark family, or model family. We did not implement robust variance estimation (Hedges et al., 2010; Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022) or multilevel meta-analysis (Van den Noortgate et al., 2013) in this version. The non-aggregated sensitivity (k = 36) partially addresses but does not solve this limitation.

2.6 Risk of bias

We conducted an informal risk-of-bias assessment adapted from ROB 2 concepts (Sterne et al., 2019), with domains for selection, performance/compute parity, reporting, and attrition. This is not a validated instrument for LLM benchmark studies; it should be understood as a structured quality checklist. Of 41 studies, 25 (61%) were rated high risk of bias, predominantly due to uncontrolled compute: multi-agent systems typically consumed more inference tokens without any single-agent scaling comparison. Of the 41 extracted rows, only 10 are independently verified (4 verified, 6 verified-corrected); 2 are unverifiable (source data could not be located); 29 remain unaudited. Among the 12 rows that were audited, 6 required correction (50% error rate). Because 31 of 41 rows (76%) lack independent verification, the pooled estimate is substantially exposed to extraction error.

2.7 Publication bias

Funnel plot inspection, Egger's regression test (Egger et al., 1997), Begg's rank correlation (Begg & Mazumdar, 1994), and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill (Duval & Tweedie, 2000) were conducted as exploratory publication-bias diagnostics. These methods are unreliable under extreme heterogeneity (I² > 90%), as funnel-plot asymmetry may reflect genuine heterogeneity or small-study effects rather than publication bias per se (Egger et al., 1997).

The formal effect size is log-OR = 0.45 (95% CI [0.23, 0.67], p < .001) for the full sample and 0.46 [0.22, 0.71] for the primary accuracy-only subset (k = 37), corresponding to OR ≈ 1.57. On the absolute scale the gain is small and weighting-dependent: an aggregate (precision/n-weighted) risk difference of ≈3 pp (Cohen's h = 0.07), versus an unweighted study mean of ≈8 pp (mean h = 0.20, right at the 0.20 "small" boundary; Cohen, 1988). The aggregate figure is pulled down by very large, near-null general-knowledge benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, n = 14,042); the gap between the weighted and unweighted summaries is itself a symptom of the extreme heterogeneity. Whether a few additional correct answers per 100 questions justify the added complexity and compute cost is a system-design question, not a statistical one.

Critical caveat: the main limitation is not the random-effects model; it is the quality and auditability of the extraction table. The pooled effect is positive but fragile: (1) The prediction interval [−0.74, 1.64] crosses zero, so a new study could plausibly find single-agent superiority. (2) Egger's test is significant (p = .024), indicating funnel asymmetry and possible publication bias or small-study effects. (3) The absolute gain is small (aggregate Cohen's h = 0.07; unweighted study mean 0.20). (4) 61% of studies are high-RoB and 76% of rows lack independent verification. (5) I² = 96.1% means the "average effect" is nearly meaningless as a point prediction.

3.4 Compute parity: the most important sensitivity

Under compute parity (k = 9), the relative effect attenuates ~31% (log-OR = 0.31, 95% CI [0.006, 0.61]); the CI lower bound barely escapes zero, so the relative conclusion reverses under a trivially different sample. On the absolute scale, however, the parity subset does not shrink: its aggregate RD is ≈5 pp (Cohen's h ≈ 0.11), larger than the full-sample ≈3 pp, because the parity studies happen to exclude the large near-null general-knowledge benchmarks that dilute the full-sample aggregate. The two scales diverge, and this divergence — not a single "≈2 pp" figure (which v6 reported in error) — is the honest takeaway: the relative multi-agent advantage is fragile under fair compute comparison, while the absolute gain stays small but is scale- and subset-dependent.

3.5 Verified subset as credibility anchor

The source-located/verified subset (k = 10 — studies whose primary source was independently located and whose reported proportions were cross-checked) yields log-OR = 0.35 [0.19, 0.51], notably lower than the full sample, with sharply reduced heterogeneity (τ² = 0.01 vs. 0.34). The lower point estimate is a warning: the 31 unverified rows may contain extraction errors that inflate the pooled effect. The reduced τ² suggests some apparent heterogeneity in the full sample arises from extraction noise rather than genuine between-study variation.

3.6 Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity is extreme: I² = 96.1%, Q(40) = 1016.6 (p < .001), τ² = 0.34, τ = 0.58. The between-study standard deviation (τ = 0.58) exceeds the pooled estimate itself (0.45), confirming that the distribution of true effects is wide. The pooled mean should not be used as a point prediction for any specific setting.

4. Discussion

4.1 Principal findings

Multi-agent LLM systems appear to outperform single-agent baselines on average (RQ1; log-OR = 0.45 full / 0.46 accuracy-only), but the absolute margin is small: an aggregate ≈3 additional correct answers per 100 items (Cohen's h = 0.07), or ≈8 as an unweighted study mean (h = 0.20). The prediction interval [−0.74, 1.64] crosses zero, meaning multi-agent superiority is not guaranteed for any new setting. For many applications, this may not justify the added infrastructure complexity and inference cost.

The Egger test (p = .024) indicates that this estimate is likely inflated by small-study effects or publication bias. The verified subset (k = 10, log-OR = 0.35) provides the most credible anchor: it shows a smaller effect with dramatically less heterogeneity, suggesting extraction noise may inflate both the estimate and the apparent between-study variation.

4.2 When might multi-agent work?

The subgroup analyses suggest a descriptive pattern, though not a causal conclusion. Code generation (log-OR = 0.83) may benefit because software engineering is inherently modular: generating code, writing tests, reviewing, and debugging map onto distinct agent roles (Hong et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Medical reasoning (log-OR = 0.42) may benefit from cooperative multi-round consensus among role-specialized expert agents (Tang et al., 2024). General knowledge shows no benefit (log-OR = 0.02). However, these subgroup differences are confounded with compute allocation, backbone model, and study quality.

4.3 The compute confound

Under compute parity the relative effect shrinks ~31% (0.45→0.31), so on the relative scale the advantage barely survives fair comparison; on the absolute scale, though, the parity subset's aggregate RD (≈5 pp) does not fall below the full sample (see §3.4), so the attenuation is scale-specific. A multi-agent debate consuming 5× the tokens of a single agent should be compared against a single agent given the same token budget via chain-of-thought, self-consistency, or repeated sampling. Zhang et al. (2025) demonstrate exactly this — multi-agent debate often fails to beat single-agent chain-of-thought and self-consistency under equal or greater inference compute. (More narrowly, Sprague et al. (2024) find that chain-of-thought's gains are concentrated in math and symbolic reasoning — a reminder that inference-time strategies are domain-specific.) That the relative multi-agent advantage barely survives compute equalization weakens the practical case for orchestration as a general default.

4.4 Limitations

Data-quality limitations. First, this is an exploratory convenience-sample meta-analysis, not a registered systematic review. The 65 excluded records without extractable data may not be missing at random (Appendix D). Second, Egger's test is significant (p = .024), indicating publication bias or small-study effects. Third, 61% of studies are high risk of bias (primarily uncontrolled compute), and only 10 of 41 rows are independently verified (76% unverified), with a 50% correction rate. Fourth, I² = 96.1% is extreme; the pooled mean is nearly meaningless as a point prediction. Fifth, metric heterogeneity (accuracy, pass@1, win rate, resolve rate) means the log-OR model assumes more homogeneity than the underlying data provide. Sixth, because item-level paired correctness was unavailable, MA and SA proportions were treated as independent binomial counts; where arms are item-paired this overstates the within-study variance (conservative), so it does not understate uncertainty — the under-estimation of overall uncertainty instead stems from the unmodeled dependency among effect sizes noted in the Seventh limitation.

Analytical limitations. Seventh, dependency among effect sizes is not formally modeled; robust variance estimation (Hedges et al., 2010) or multilevel modeling (Van den Noortgate et al., 2013) would be needed for rigorous inference. Eighth, all subgroup analyses are exploratory and uncorrected for multiplicity. Ninth, no moderator is significant in meta-regression, but the regression is underpowered.

5. Conclusion

This project does not establish that multi-agent LLM systems generally outperform single-agent baselines. It suggests a small positive average effect in the current convenience sample (log-OR = 0.45 full / 0.46 accuracy-only; aggregate RD ≈3 pp, Cohen's h = 0.07), but the evidence is highly uncertain: heterogeneity is extreme, the prediction interval crosses zero, the absolute effect is small and weighting-dependent, publication bias is detected, and most extraction rows are unverified. Under compute parity the relative effect attenuates to log-OR 0.31 (statistically borderline), even though the absolute risk difference does not shrink.

The most practical takeaway is that multi-agent systems should be compared against compute-matched single-agent baselines before claiming synergy. Multi-agent orchestration may help for decomposable, verifiable tasks (code generation, medical reasoning) under generous compute budgets, but the evidence base is too contaminated to support strong claims. The field urgently needs compute-controlled comparisons, independent extraction audits, and registered meta-analyses.

Appendix A: Reproducibility

All analyses were conducted using Python 3.12 managed via uv. The full pipeline is executed by a single entry point: 

uv sync && uv run python main.py

Code and data are maintained in a Git repository and were not included in this HTML export. The repository contains data/, src/, main.py, pyproject.toml, uv.lock, and report/assets/. Key components:

  • Entry point: main.py runs the full pipeline end-to-end, reading data from data/, executing all analyses via modules in src/, and writing figures and summary outputs to report/assets/.

  • Data: Raw search results in data/raw/; extracted and verified effect-size tables in data/extracted/ and data/verified/.
  • Figures: All figures exported to report/assets/ with stable filenames.
  • Output validation: main.py and the v7 notebook include runtime validation checks (subgroup tables sum to k = 41; the report's RD and Cohen's h equal the pipeline outputs; n-items-provenance assertions) — all passing.
  • Tests: 18 unit tests in tests/test_stats.py. Run via uv run pytest tests/.

Appendix E: Cohen's h as Secondary Effect Size

Cohen's h = 2 arcsin(√pMA) − 2 arcsin(√pSA) provides an absolute-scale effect size that is less sensitive to base-rate inflation than log-OR. In high-accuracy regimes (>90% for both conditions), a small proportion difference produces a large log-OR but a small h. Benchmarks: 0.20 = small, 0.50 = medium, 0.80 = large (Cohen, 1988). v7 note: the values below are the aggregate (summed-proportion) h — the directly reproducible pooled value, emitted by src/effect_sizes.pooled_absolute_effect. The unweighted mean of per-study h runs higher (0.20 for the full sample); the report foregrounds the aggregate value but reports both, since their gap reflects the I² = 96% heterogeneity.

More Agents, Better Results? An Exploratory Convenience-Sample Meta-Analysis of Multi-Agent vs Single-Agent LLM Systems

Xinyu Yang (r1020926)
KU Leuven, Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences
Meta-Analysis (G0B75a), Academic Year 2025–2026
26 May 2026 — Revision 6 (post v5 peer review)

Abstract

This exploratory convenience-sample meta-analysis synthesizes 41 within-study comparisons (2023–2026) of multi-agent LLM orchestration versus single-agent prompting on benchmark accuracy. Multi-agent systems improved performance by approximately 3–5 percentage points in the full sample and around 2 percentage points under compute equivalence. The formal effect size was log-OR = 0.45, 95% CI [0.23, 0.67] (REML with HKSJ adjustment), but the estimate is fragile: heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 96.1%), the prediction interval crossed zero ([−0.74, 1.64]), Cohen's h was trivial (0.08), and Egger's test detected funnel asymmetry (p = .024). The verified subset (k = 10) yielded a lower estimate (log-OR = 0.35) with sharply reduced heterogeneity. Because 76% of extraction rows lack independent verification (50% error rate among audited rows), and most studies did not control compute, these findings are provisional and hypothesis-generating.

1. Introduction

The multi-agent paradigm for large language models rests on a compelling intuition: just as human teams outperform individuals on complex tasks, multiple LLM instances collaborating through structured protocols should exceed what a single instance achieves alone. Early work by Du et al. (2023) demonstrated that multiagent debate improved factuality and reasoning in ChatGPT, while Liang et al. (2024) showed that multi-agent discussion encouraged divergent thinking. Frameworks such as AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023), MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2023), and ChatDev (Qian et al., 2024) now orchestrate dozens of specialized agents in hierarchies, debates, and cooperative workflows.

Yet a skeptical counter-narrative has emerged. Zhang et al. (2025) directly show that multi-agent debate often fails to outperform simple single-agent baselines such as chain-of-thought and self-consistency, even while consuming substantially more inference-time computation. As frontier models improve, the coordination overhead may outweigh diminishing marginal gains.

To our knowledge, no quantitative meta-analysis has yet synthesized these conflicting findings; our non-exhaustive search did not locate a directly comparable study, though multi-agent LLM surveys and benchmarks exist (Guo et al., 2024). Individual studies vary in architecture (debate vs. hierarchy vs. cooperation), task domain (code vs. medical vs. math), backbone model, and whether compute parity is enforced. This heterogeneity makes narrative reviews unreliable. We address four research questions:

RQ1 (Primary). Do multi-agent LLM systems outperform single-agent baselines on benchmark accuracy?
RQ2 (Compute parity). Under matched compute budgets, does the multi-agent advantage survive?
RQ3 (Frontier paradox). As baseline single-agent accuracy increases, does the orchestration gain shrink?
RQ4 (Moderators). Which task and architecture properties predict effect direction?

2. Methods

2.1 Search strategy and limitations

We deployed six parallel search agents covering distinct topic clusters: multi-agent debate, cooperative/hierarchical architectures, SWE and code generation, medical/scientific reasoning, critical and null-result literature, and frontier-model comparisons. Each agent queried arXiv, ACL Anthology, NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR proceedings, and Google Scholar, targeting papers from 2023–2026. This yielded 153 records. Search strings are documented in Appendix C.

Important caveat: This is a convenience sample, not a registered systematic review. No protocol was pre-registered. The search strategy, while broad, cannot guarantee exhaustive coverage. The six-agent approach introduces selection biases that differ from but are not necessarily better than manual systematic searching. Results should be treated as hypothesis-generating.

2.2 Screening and inclusion (PICOS)

Population: Empirical 2023–2026 LLM studies. Intervention: Any multi-agent orchestration (debate, cooperation, hierarchy, role-play, verifier-critic, mixture-of-agents, planner-executor). Comparator: Single-agent baseline using the same backbone model. Outcome: Accuracy or success-rate proportion on a named benchmark. Study design: Within-study head-to-head comparison. Only studies reporting comparable MA and SA outcomes on the same benchmark, using the same or clearly comparable backbone model, were eligible.

Exclusions: classical multi-agent reinforcement learning, position papers without empirical results, studies lacking a single-agent comparator, and simulation-only evaluations. After deduplication (26 removed) and PICOS screening (21 excluded), 106 records remained. Of these, 41 reported extractable accuracy data for both multi-agent and single-agent conditions. The 65 excluded records are catalogued in Appendix D. Screening was performed by the author with AI assistance; no formal inter-rater reliability was computed, which is a limitation.

For each of the 41 included studies, an AI extraction agent read the paper (abstract, results tables, or full text via arXiv) and produced a structured row with the following fields:

Verification process. An independent verification agent audited 12 of 41 rows (29%, selected with seed 42). It located each paper on arXiv, cross-checked the reported numbers against the original tables, and flagged discrepancies. Of the 12 audited rows, 4 were verified without changes, 6 required correction (50% error rate), and 2 could not be independently located. The dominant error pattern was denominator miscoding: three studies (S009, S025, S040) used n-items = 100 as a percentage normalization when the real benchmark had 300 or 805 items. The underlying percentages were correct, so the effect-size direction was preserved, but the precision of the log-OR changed after correction. Other corrections involved agent counts (the extraction agent defaulted to 3) and one compute-parity misclassification. All corrections are documented in data/verified/verification-report.md. The corrected table (data/verified/effect-size-table.csv) is the input to all analyses. Inter-rater reliability on audited rows: binary field kappa = 0.91; count-field ICC > 0.95.

Remaining risk. The 29 unaudited rows (71%) may contain similar errors. The verified-only sensitivity analysis (k = 10, Section 3.5) provides the most credible anchor for the pooled estimate.

2.4 Effect size

We computed log odds ratios (log-OR) as the primary effect size. Because item-level paired correctness was unavailable, MA and SA proportions were treated as independent binomial counts. This likely underestimates uncertainty. A 0.5 continuity correction was applied conditionally, only to studies with zero cells (Sweeting et al., 2004); none were present. For each study, we extracted n-items (total benchmark questions), n-correct under multi-agent, and n-correct under single-agent conditions. Where n-items could not be extracted directly, it was estimated from reported percentages and total benchmark sizes. Of the 41 studies, 13 have imputed or estimated denominators (k = 28 exact). The full extraction table is in Appendix Table A1.

Risk-difference approximation. To aid practical interpretation, we report approximate risk differences (RD = pMA − pSA) as the primary interpretive metric. The pooled RD across all 41 studies is approximately 3–5 percentage points. Under compute parity, this shrinks to approximately 2 percentage points. Log-OR is retained as the formal effect size for statistical modeling.

Metric heterogeneity. The 41 comparisons include accuracy, pass@1, win rate, executability scores, and resolve rates. We treated all as binomial proportions, but acknowledge this conflation is a simplification. Win-rate benchmarks (e.g., AlpacaEval) operate under different assumptions than item-level accuracy; results should be interpreted with this caveat.

2.5 Statistical model

The primary analysis used a restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) random-effects model (Viechtbauer, 2005) with a modified Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment (Hartung & Knapp, 2001; IntHout et al., 2014). The HKSJ adjustment computes the scale factor s²HK = Σwi(yi − μ)²/(k−1) and applies max(1, s²HK) as a variance inflation floor, following the conservative recommendation that HKSJ should never narrow CIs relative to the naïve inverse-variance estimate (Röver et al., 2015). CIs use t(k−1) degrees of freedom. Prediction intervals use t(k−2) following IntHout et al. (2016). DerSimonian-Laird estimation served as a sensitivity check. Cochran's Q is computed using fixed-effect weights and the fixed-effect pooled mean, yielding a single Q value identical across variance estimators (Borenstein et al., 2009, Ch. 16).

Subgroup analyses used separate random-effects models per level with Qbetween computed via the partition-of-Q approach (Qbetween = Qtotal − ΣQwithin; Borenstein et al., 2009, Ch. 19). Singleton categories (factuality k = 1 for task; moa and planner-executor k = 1 each for architecture) were merged into the nearest "other" category so that all subgroup tables sum to k = 41. Meta-regression was mixed-effects with conditional REML τ² estimation and Knapp-Hartung t-distribution inference (Viechtbauer, 2010). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses re-estimate τ² per iteration.

Dependency. Multiple effect sizes sometimes derive from the same paper, benchmark family, or model family. We did not implement robust variance estimation (Hedges et al., 2010; Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022) or multilevel meta-analysis (Van den Noortgate et al., 2013) in this version. The non-aggregated sensitivity (k = 36) partially addresses but does not solve this limitation.

2.6 Risk of bias

We conducted an informal risk-of-bias assessment adapted from ROB 2 concepts (Sterne et al., 2019), with domains for selection, performance/compute parity, reporting, and attrition. This is not a validated instrument for LLM benchmark studies; it should be understood as a structured quality checklist. Of 41 studies, 25 (61%) were rated high risk of bias, predominantly due to uncontrolled compute: multi-agent systems typically consumed more inference tokens without any single-agent scaling comparison. Of the 41 extracted rows, only 10 are independently verified (4 verified, 6 verified-corrected); 2 are unverifiable (source data could not be located); 29 remain unaudited. Among the 12 rows that were audited, 6 required correction (50% error rate). Because 31 of 41 rows (76%) lack independent verification, the pooled estimate is substantially exposed to extraction error.

2.7 Publication bias

Funnel plot inspection, Egger's regression test (Egger et al., 1997), Begg's rank correlation (Begg & Mazumdar, 1994), and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill (Duval & Tweedie, 2000) were conducted as exploratory publication-bias diagnostics. These methods are unreliable under extreme heterogeneity (I² > 90%), as funnel-plot asymmetry may reflect genuine heterogeneity or small-study effects rather than publication bias per se (Egger et al., 1997).

3.2 Descriptive characteristics

The 41 included comparisons span 2023–2026. The dominant architecture is debate (k = 20, 49%), followed by cooperation (k = 9), hierarchy (k = 6), verifier-critic (k = 2), role-play (k = 2), and other/rare (k = 2, comprising one mixture-of-agents and one planner-executor study). Task categories include code generation (k = 11), other/mixed (k = 11, including one factuality study merged from the singleton category), medical reasoning (k = 7), math (k = 6), evaluation tasks (k = 3), and general knowledge (k = 3). Backbone models span GPT-4 variants, Claude, Gemini, Qwen, and open-source models. Of the 41 studies, 13 have imputed or estimated n-items values. Only 9 studies enforced compute parity. Multi-agent systems improved performance by approximately 3–5 percentage points on average (risk difference). The formal effect size is log-OR = 0.45 (95% CI [0.23, 0.67], p < .001), corresponding to OR = 1.57. On an absolute scale, however, the overall Cohen's h is 0.08 — well below the 0.20 threshold for a "small" effect (Cohen, 1988). Whether 3–5 additional correct answers per 100 benchmark questions justifies the added complexity and compute cost is a system-design question, not a statistical one.

Critical caveat: the main limitation is not the random-effects model; it is the quality and auditability of the extraction table. The pooled effect is positive but fragile: (1) The prediction interval [−0.74, 1.64] crosses zero, so a new study could plausibly find single-agent superiority. (2) Egger's test is significant (p = .024), indicating funnel asymmetry and possible publication bias or small-study effects. (3) Cohen's h = 0.08 is trivial on the absolute scale. (4) 61% of studies are high-RoB and 76% of rows lack independent verification. (5) I² = 96.1% means the "average effect" is nearly meaningless as a point prediction.

3.4 Compute parity: the most important sensitivity

Under compute parity (k = 9), the effect shrinks to approximately 2 percentage points (log-OR = 0.31, 95% CI [0.006, 0.61]; Cohen's h ≈ 0.05, negligible). The CI lower bound barely escapes zero; the conclusion reverses under a trivially different sample. This is the most practically important finding: multi-agent systems under fair compute comparisons gain roughly 2 additional correct items per 100 benchmark questions, an effect that is statistically borderline and practically trivial.

3.5 Verified subset as credibility anchor

The verified subset (k = 10) yields log-OR = 0.35 [0.19, 0.51], notably lower than the full sample, with sharply reduced heterogeneity (τ² = 0.01 vs. 0.34). The lower point estimate is a warning: the 31 unverified rows may contain extraction errors that inflate the pooled effect. The reduced τ² suggests some apparent heterogeneity in the full sample arises from extraction noise rather than genuine between-study variation.

3.6 Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity is extreme: I² = 96.1%, Q(40) = 1016.6 (p < .001), τ² = 0.34, τ = 0.58. The between-study standard deviation (τ = 0.58) exceeds the pooled estimate itself (0.45), confirming that the distribution of true effects is wide. The pooled mean should not be used as a point prediction for any specific setting.

4. Discussion

4.1 Principal findings

Multi-agent LLM systems appear to outperform single-agent baselines on average (RQ1), but by a trivial absolute margin: approximately 3–5 additional correct answers per 100 benchmark items (log-OR = 0.45, Cohen's h = 0.08). The prediction interval [−0.74, 1.64] crosses zero, meaning multi-agent superiority is not guaranteed for any new setting. For many applications, this may not justify the added infrastructure complexity and inference cost.

The Egger test (p = .024) indicates that this estimate is likely inflated by small-study effects or publication bias. The verified subset (k = 10, log-OR = 0.35) provides the most credible anchor: it shows a smaller effect with dramatically less heterogeneity, suggesting extraction noise may inflate both the estimate and the apparent between-study variation.

4.2 When might multi-agent work?

The subgroup analyses suggest a descriptive pattern, though not a causal conclusion. Code generation (log-OR = 0.83) may benefit because software engineering is inherently modular: generating code, writing tests, reviewing, and debugging map onto distinct agent roles (Hong et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Medical reasoning (log-OR = 0.42) may benefit from adversarial verification, where a second agent challenges the first's diagnosis (Tang et al., 2024). General knowledge shows no benefit (log-OR = 0.02). However, these subgroup differences are confounded with compute allocation, backbone model, and study quality.

4.3 The compute confound

Under compute parity, the effect shrinks by ~31% (from 0.45 to 0.31), and the absolute gain becomes approximately 2 percentage points (Cohen's h ≈ 0.05, negligible). This is the most practically important finding. A multi-agent debate consuming 5x the tokens of a single agent should be compared against a single agent given the same token budget via chain-of-thought, self-consistency, or repeated sampling. Zhang et al. (2025) demonstrate this directly; Sprague et al. (2024) show more broadly that inference-time computation scaling can substitute for multi-step reasoning strategies. That the multi-agent advantage barely survives after compute equalization substantially weakens the practical case for multi-agent orchestration as a general strategy.

4.4 Limitations

Data-quality limitations. First, this is an exploratory convenience-sample meta-analysis, not a registered systematic review. The 65 excluded records without extractable data may not be missing at random (Appendix D). Second, Egger's test is significant (p = .024), indicating publication bias or small-study effects. Third, 61% of studies are high risk of bias (primarily uncontrolled compute), and only 10 of 41 rows are independently verified (76% unverified), with a 50% correction rate. Fourth, I² = 96.1% is extreme; the pooled mean is nearly meaningless as a point prediction. Fifth, metric heterogeneity (accuracy, pass@1, win rate, resolve rate) means the log-OR model assumes more homogeneity than the underlying data provide. Sixth, because item-level paired correctness was unavailable, MA and SA proportions were treated as independent binomial counts, likely underestimating uncertainty.

Analytical limitations. Seventh, dependency among effect sizes is not formally modeled; robust variance estimation (Hedges et al., 2010) or multilevel modeling (Van den Noortgate et al., 2013) would be needed for rigorous inference. Eighth, all subgroup analyses are exploratory and uncorrected for multiplicity. Ninth, no moderator is significant in meta-regression, but the regression is underpowered.

5. Conclusion

This project does not establish that multi-agent LLM systems generally outperform single-agent baselines. It suggests a small positive average effect in the current convenience sample (approximately 3–5 percentage points; log-OR = 0.45), but the evidence is highly uncertain: heterogeneity is extreme, the prediction interval crosses zero, the absolute effect is trivial (Cohen's h = 0.08), publication bias is detected, and most extraction rows are unverified. Under compute parity, the effect shrinks to approximately 2 percentage points — statistically borderline and practically negligible.

The most practical takeaway is that multi-agent systems should be compared against compute-matched single-agent baselines before claiming synergy. Multi-agent orchestration may help for decomposable, verifiable tasks (code generation, medical reasoning) under generous compute budgets, but the evidence base is too contaminated to support strong claims. The field urgently needs compute-controlled comparisons, independent extraction audits, and registered meta-analyses.

Appendix E: Cohen's h as Secondary Effect Size

Cohen's h = 2 arcsin(√pMA) − 2 arcsin(√pSA) provides an absolute-scale effect size that is less sensitive to base-rate inflation than log-OR. In high-accuracy regimes (>90% for both conditions), a small proportion difference produces a large log-OR but a small h. Benchmarks: 0.20 = small, 0.50 = medium, 0.80 = large (Cohen, 1988).
